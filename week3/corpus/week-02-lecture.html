Say plainly: you have already done the hard part, which is noticing. Today is vocabulary.

Training and inference are different things

Training — months, once, enormous cost. Produces a fixed set of weights.

Inference — the two seconds after you press enter. The weights do not change.

Nothing you type ever teaches the model anything.

4

≈4 min.

This is the misconception most worth killing early, because it underpins the whole course. Students routinely believe that correcting the model in a chat "teaches" it.

It does not. Within a conversation, earlier turns are re-sent as context — which is why it appears to learn, and why the illusion collapses the moment you start a new chat.

Link forward: this is also why memory (Week 9) is an engineering problem rather than a model feature. If the model cannot learn, something else has to remember.

Tokens

5

Section divider. ≈14 min for this block — the genuinely surprising part of the session, and worth the time.

The model does not see words

Three rows to point at, in this order:

  1. Ukrainian 17 vs English 13 — same meaning, near-identical character count, 31% more tokens.
  2. The long number — 23 tokens for 22 characters, about one per digit.

23 tokens for one number

   3.14159265358979323846   →   23 tokens

Ask the room: if this is what a number looks like to the model, why is it bad at arithmetic?

8

≈2 min. The most striking row on the table — give it its own beat.

The model never sees "the number 3.14159…". It sees a couple of dozen fragments and has to do arithmetic across them. Digits do not arrive as quantities.

This is the honest, mechanical answer to a question students ask constantly — why can it write an essay but not multiply? — and it is far better than "it's just not good at maths."

Why fragments, though?

One token per word? The vocabulary is a fixed table — one row per token. You cannot hold every word, name, typo and identifier in every language. Anything missing becomes unrepresentable.

One token per character? Nothing is ever missing — but sequences get long, and attention compares every token with every other, so cost grows with roughly the square of length.

9

≈3 min. Pose it as a design problem with two obvious answers that both fail, then give the compromise on the next slide.

Per-word also wastes the table: run, runs, running would be three unrelated rows sharing nothing.

Per-character also burns model capacity on relearning that c-o-n-t-e-x-t spells a word, before any actual reasoning can start.

Keep it at this level. BPE internals and vocabulary sizes are a rabbit hole and nothing later in the course depends on them.

Sub-word pieces — the compromise

Start from characters. Repeatedly merge the most frequent adjacent pair in a huge training corpus. Stop when the vocabulary is full.

Common sequences earned their own token. Rare ones never did.

Nothing is ever unrepresentable — anything unfamiliar is spelled out from smaller pieces.

10

≈3 min. This is byte-pair encoding, though the name matters less than the mechanism.

The key word is frequent. The vocabulary is the frozen result of a counting exercise over somebody's training corpus.

That single fact explains the entire table, which is the next slide.

One mechanism, every row

RowWhy
Ukrainian 17 vs English 13Counting ran on a mostly-English corpus. English sequences earned merges; Ukrainian ones largely didn't
Number → ~1 token/digitArbitrary digit strings are too varied to earn merges
gemini-3.5-flash → 9An identifier never seen in the corpus, rebuilt from fragments
antidisestablishmentarianism → 6Rare word, no merge, spelled out

Tokens aren't smaller than words because of language. They're smaller because the vocabulary was built by counting what was frequent — and your corpus wasn't what got counted.

≈3 min. The payoff slide. Walk the table, then read the quote aloud.

That reframe matters for them specifically: the Ukrainian result is not a quirk of Ukrainian, it is a consequence of whose text got counted when the vocabulary was built.

Practical consequence, worth stating plainly: their Week 3–4 ingestion will cost about a third more tokens per page than an English-language course would lead them to budget for.

Plant quietly for Week 4: identifiers shredding into many tokens is part of why dense retrieval fails on exact identifiers, and why Lab 1 requires hybrid retrieval rather than embeddings alone.

Context

13

Section divider. ≈8 min.

This is the block that connects directly to Experiment 4 — the pasted syllabus was context, and that is the whole idea.

The context window is everything it can see

System instructions · your question · conversation history · retrieved documents · tool results · memory

All of it, re-sent, every single call.

14

≈3 min.

The re-sent part surprises people. There is no session on the model's side — a conversation works because the whole history is resent each turn. That is also why long conversations get slower and more expensive.

Tie back hard to Experiment 4: pasting the syllabus in was manual retrieval. Weeks 3–4 automate exactly that, and nothing else.

Name the constraint: the window is finite. When it fills, something has to go — and choosing what is the engineering problem the rest of the course is about.

Context is not free

Every token in the window is paid for, on every call.

  • Longer prompt → higher cost, higher latency
  • More retrieved chunks → not automatically better answers
  • Irrelevant context actively hurts — it competes for attention
15

≈3 min.

Kill the "just put everything in, the window is huge" instinct now, before Week 4 when they build retrieval and are tempted to stuff it.

The third bullet is the non-obvious one and worth dwelling on: adding more context can make an answer worse, because the relevant sentence is now competing with noise. Week 4 covers this properly as a RAG failure mode; today just plant that more is not better.

Long context versus retrieval is a real engineering trade-off, not a solved question — they will ask.

18

≈3 min. The reveal. Put the names against the groups already on the board.

Say it explicitly: you derived this from five experiments and your own notes. This is not a list to memorise, it is a checklist for when an answer comes back wrong — which of these did I leave the model to guess?

That reframe is the point of the whole segment. A prompt is not "wording." It is a set of decisions, each of which you either make or leave to chance.

The one you couldn't have discovered

config=types.GenerateContentConfig(

System — standing rules, set by the developer User — the request, often from someone you don't control

19

≈4 min. This is the part the Week 1 lab could not surface, because it lives in the API, not the prompt text.

Why the separation exists: the developer's instructions should outrank whatever a user types. A user saying "ignore your instructions" should not win.

Then the uncomfortable truth, stated plainly: the separation is a convention the model was trained to respect, not a security boundary. Everything arrives as one token sequence. There is no privilege enforcement.

That is the seed of prompt injection in Week 7 — plant it in one sentence and stop. Do not teach injection today.

Controlling what comes out

20

Section divider. ≈12 min covering two controls: temperature, then structured output.

This is the designated release valve (plan §3). If the session is running long, these two merge into one brisk segment — they answer the same question.

Temperature — Experiment 5, explained

The model produces a probability distribution over next tokens. Temperature rescales it before sampling.

  • Low → sharpen. Takes its top candidate, reliably.
  • High → flatten. More variety, more risk.
  • 0 → effectively always the most likely token.
21

≈4 min.

Re-run their Experiment 5 live — same prompt three times, then three more with temperature=0. The answers converge.

config=types.GenerateContentConfig(temperature=0)

The point: last week's "it's not a function" was never a defect. It was sampling, and it has a dial.

Keep it mechanical. Resist "temperature = creativity" — it does not make the model imaginative, it makes it less likely to pick its own favourite. Mystifying this is the main failure mode of this slide.

Then the engineering question: which parts of your Lab 3 system want this near zero? Classification, extraction, routing — anything you intend to parse.

Asking nicely is not a guarantee

Experiment 3, last week:

"Return ONLY a JSON array. No prose, no code fence."

Sometimes it complied. Sometimes it wrapped it in a fence anyway.

A prompt is a request. It is not a constraint.

22

≈3 min.

Ask who got clean JSON and who didn't — the split in the room is the lesson.

Then the engineering consequence, which is the real point: code that parses this reply works fine in testing and crashes in production on the day the model adds a sentence in front of it. Politeness is not an interface contract.

Structured output is a configuration

class Risk(BaseModel):
    risk: str
    severity: str

config=types.GenerateContentConfig(
    response_mime_type="application/json",
    response_schema=list[Risk],
)

Same prompt. Now it cannot return anything else.

23

≈5 min. Run this live against the same prompt as Experiment 3 — verified working on gemini-3.5-flash:

raw text starts with: '[{"risk": "Financial loss due to unauthorized usage…' parses as JSON: True parsed count: 3

No code fence, no preamble, parses directly with json.loads.

The shift to name: they moved the requirement out of the prose and into the API call. The prompt asks; the config constrains.

Why this is core and not a nice-to-have: Week 7's tool calling is exactly this mechanism. A tool call is the model emitting a structured object that your code executes. If it can't reliably produce a schema-shaped object, it cannot call tools at all. This slide is the prerequisite for half the back end of the course.

Stage 1 — tokens in, tokens out

 (in)                              (out)

Two counters. The whole transaction is visible.

25

≈2 min. Establish the baseline deliberately — the contrast is the whole lesson.

One shot: the model predicts the answer directly, token by token, and everything it produces is something you can read.

Stage 2 — "think step by step"

Someone noticed: ask a model to reason before answering, and it gets markedly better at hard problems.

Wei et al., "Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models," Google, 2022

But the reasoning was ordinary visible output — you typed the request, you paid output rates for it, and you read past it to find the answer.

26

≈3 min. arXiv 2201.11903.

Worth noting the pattern: this came out of Google, like the attention paper from last week. Both mechanisms, published openly, that everyone then built on.

The key framing for the next slide: chain-of-thought was a prompting trick. It was the user's job to ask for it, and the reasoning cluttered the answer.

Then pose the obvious question and let it hang: if this reliably helps, why make the user ask?

Stage 3 — the loop moved inside

prompt ──▶ [ internal reasoning ] ──▶ answer
 (in)        (thinking — hidden)       (out)

Three counters. The thinking is invisible in response.text, billed at output rates, and — unlike the prompting trick — controllable.

27

≈3 min. The payoff.

The sentence that makes it click:

Chain-of-thought went from something you type into the prompt, to something the model does on its own and doesn't show you. Same mechanism — relocated, and hidden.

And the callback to Week 1, which keeps this from feeling like new machinery: last week we said a model predicts the next token, feeds it back, and repeats. A reasoning model runs that same loop twice — once privately to think, once publicly to answer.

"Test-time compute" is the name for spending compute at answer time rather than training time.

What it actually costs

Four runs. Identical trivial prompt — "reply with exactly: setup complete":

prompt=7   thinking=71    answer=2
prompt=7   thinking=80    answer=2
prompt=7   thinking=95    answer=2
prompt=7   thinking=102   answer=2

35–50× more thinking than answer — on a prompt with nothing to reason about.

Three points, in order:

  1. Those tokens are billed. They were there last week and nobody saw them.
  2. They are invisible by default — response.text shows none of it.
  3. The count varies by a third between identical runs. You cannot budget by assuming.

≈3 min. Open a price list live if you can:

Two things to point at:

Output costs 4–5× input, at every provider. That is the shape of LLM pricing, and thinking tokens are billed as output.

Google states it explicitly; the other two don't mention reasoning tokens on their pricing pages at all. Name that out loud — it is the same lesson as the demo, one level up: the cost is invisible in the response and largely invisible in the documentation. A developer who never prints usage_metadata has no reason to learn this exists until the bill arrives.

Order-of-magnitude, if they want it: ~1,500 calls/student/semester × 25 students is on the order of $10–15 of pure invisible thinking at Flash rates. Small — which is exactly why it hides. Week 11 makes it a real cost problem; today it only needs to be visible.

A dial, not a switch

thinking_level — low · medium · high

More thinking costs money and latency. A classification call does not need what a multi-step proof needs.

30

≈2 min.

Do not let the room conclude that more thinking is simply better — that is the wrong lesson and it will cost them in Lab 3, where an agent turn is several calls and latency compounds.

The judgement — how much reasoning does this particular call actually need? — is Week 11's material. Today just establish that it is adjustable, and that the default is not always right.

Where it goes wrong

31

Section divider. ≈10 min.

They have already seen this — Experiment 4, round one. Now it gets causes.

Hallucination has causes

CauseWhat it looks like
Nothing in contextConfident invention — Experiment 4, round one
Wrong thing retrievedFluent answer, grounded in the wrong source
Stale training dataTrue once, not any more
Conflicting sourcesPicks one, doesn't flag the conflict
Plausible-shape completionA citation, URL or method name that looks perfect and doesn't exist
32

≈4 min.

The last row is the one this audience feels personally — everyone has had a model invent a library method that should exist. Explain it mechanically: the model learned the shape of an API call, a DOI, a URL. Generating a well-formed one is exactly what next-token prediction does well. Being right requires knowing something it was never told.

The reframe worth landing: these are different problems with different fixes. "Nothing in context" is fixed by retrieval (Week 4). "Wrong thing retrieved" is fixed by better retrieval and evaluation (Weeks 4, 11). "Plausible-shape" is barely fixed at all — you verify against a real source.

Treating hallucination as one undifferentiated flaw is what leads people to think a better model will solve it.

The honest position

There is no setting that turns hallucination off.

What you can do: ground it · constrain the output · give it an exit · measure it

"If the sources do not answer the question, reply: not stated."

33

≈3 min.

Each one maps to a week: grounding is 3–4, constraining is today's structured output, measuring is Week 11.

The exit clause is the cheapest and most overlooked. Point back at Experiment 4 — their Week 1 notebook told the model to say "not stated" if the text didn't answer. That one line is the difference between a system that admits ignorance and one that fills the gap. It costs nothing.

Close the block honestly: the model did exactly what it does. With nothing to work from, confident invention is the expected output, not a malfunction. That is a design constraint to engineer around, not a bug awaiting a patch.
